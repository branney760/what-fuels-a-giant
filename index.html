HARBOR POINT FIELD NOTE • BIOLOGICAL MOLECULES

What Fuels a Giant?

Investigate food molecules, then help Maya explain how tiny prey support a blue whale.

Maya’s new exhibit question

Harbor Point’s visitors know blue whales eat krill. Maya asks: “What does a whale actually get from its food?” Her draft says, “Krill are just packets of fat. A whale only needs energy, so other biological molecules do not matter.” Your job is to test that explanation.

Teaching model: Harbor Point and every sample result here are fictional. The tests demonstrate biochemical principles; they are not measurements of wild krill composition or whale dietary requirements. No animals are sampled. All test procedures are virtual.

Allow about 25–35 minutes. You will learn the molecule groups, check controls, test three prepared samples, and write a Field Note. This investigation stands on its own from the seawater acidification activity.

Before you begin: Four groups, many functions

A monomer is a small building unit. A polymer is a larger molecule made of linked repeating units. Proteins and nucleic acids are polymers; polysaccharides are carbohydrate polymers. Simple sugars are carbohydrates too, but they are not large polymers. Lipids are grouped with biological macromolecules even though they are not repeating-monomer polymers.

Connect building units to functions
GroupBuilding units / examplesFunctions in an animal
CarbohydratesMonosaccharides such as glucose; polysaccharides include glycogen and chitin.Fuel, energy storage, and structural materials. Krill exoskeletons contain chitin.
ProteinsAmino acids linked in polypeptides.Enzymes, transport, muscle contraction, structure and repair.
LipidsFats contain glycerol and fatty acids; phospholipids and steroids are other examples.Energy storage, cell membranes and signaling. Lipid-rich blubber also contributes to insulation.
Nucleic acidsNucleotides linked in DNA and RNA.Store and use genetic information; RNA participates in protein production.

During digestion, hydrolysis uses water to break many food molecules into smaller components. Cells absorb and process those components, using them for energy or to build their own molecules. The whale does not simply transfer intact krill proteins into its muscles. Matter is rearranged; energy is transferred, used and ultimately dissipated as heat.

Why DNA belongs in a food investigation

Krill cells contain DNA and RNA, as do whale cells. Food nucleic acids can be digested into smaller components. Eating krill DNA does not turn a whale into a krill. The tests on this bench do not detect DNA or RNA, so you must distinguish biological background knowledge from a measured result.

1. Plan your investigation

Three prepared extracts await testing. Sample preparation and detection thresholds matter; the results describe these fictional extracts, not all material present in an entire organism.

  • Krill extract: a fictional prepared prey sample.
  • Phytoplankton extract: a fictional mixed algal sample, representing primary producers in a marine food web.
  • Exhibit mix X: a prepared mixture Maya wants to use in a demonstration. Its ingredients will be revealed after testing.

For each assay, run known controls before testing samples. The positive control contains the target; the negative control is distilled water. A valid positive control confirms the assay can respond, and the negative control checks for an unexpected background response.

2. Explore the virtual test bench

Choose an assay and run its controls.

Tube drawings are symbols, not photographs. Positive (+) and negative (−) labels accompany colors. Heating, mixing, staining and timing are completed virtually; this is not a home laboratory protocol.

What each assay can and cannot tell you
  • Benedict’s detects certain reducing sugars after heating, not all carbohydrates.
  • Iodine detects starch. A negative result does not rule out glycogen, chitin or other carbohydrates.
  • Biuret responds to peptide bonds, supporting the presence of proteins or sufficiently long peptides. It does not identify a particular protein.
  • Sudan IV stains lipid-rich material. We display a simplified staining result in a symbolic tube.
  • These qualitative tests do not measure grams, calories, digestibility, DNA, or a complete nutritional profile. Color intensity here is not a concentration scale.

3. Your evidence table

Run all four assays on all three samples (12 results). Results appear only after you run each test. Repeating a test returns the fixed model result, not an independent replicate.

Reveal exhibit mix X after completing its tests

Complete all four assays on exhibit mix X to unlock its ingredients.

Check the claim: If the krill extract tests positive for protein and lipid but negative for reducing sugars and starch, what is supported? Report what each assay detected. Do not conclude that krill contain no carbohydrates or nucleic acids.

4. From prey molecules to whale biology

Blue whales primarily feed on krill. Krill and other consumers obtain organic matter through marine food webs, while primary producers use light energy to build organic molecules from inorganic carbon. Food supplies both chemical energy and matter. It is not enough to say “a whale needs calories”: its cells also need suitable materials for growth, repair and normal function.

Review the limits after writing

You would need quantitative composition, digestibility and energy measurements for an energy comparison. An ecosystem claim also requires evidence about prey abundance, distribution and whale access. These tests compare detection in samples, not population change. Changing pH could affect organisms, but this activity does not test that relationship.
